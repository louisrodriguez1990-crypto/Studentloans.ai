Refinancing your federal student loans can lower your interest rate and monthly payment — but it comes with a permanent trade-off: you give up federal protections that could be worth far more than the rate savings.

**As of April 2026:** With the SAVE Plan blocked by a court injunction and uncertainty around income-driven repayment, some borrowers are reconsidering refinancing. This guide explains when it makes sense and when it doesn't.

---

## What refinancing means

When you refinance federal student loans, a private lender pays off your federal loans and issues you a new private loan at a new rate. From that point forward, your loans are private. You lose access to:

- **Income-driven repayment plans** (IBR, PAYE, SAVE)
- **Public Service Loan Forgiveness (PSLF)**
- **Federal forbearance and deferment protections**
- **IDR forgiveness** after 20–25 years
- **Income-driven payment adjustments** if you lose your job

There is no way to move back to federal loans after refinancing.

---

## When refinancing may make sense

Refinancing is worth serious consideration if all of the following are true:

1. **You work in the private sector** — PSLF requires government or nonprofit employment. If you'll never qualify, refinancing doesn't sacrifice that benefit.
2. **Your income is stable and high** — If your income is significantly above the standard payment threshold, IDR plans likely won't save you money anyway.
3. **You have no plans to pursue forgiveness** — If your loan balance is small relative to your income, you'll pay it off before any forgiveness kicks in.
4. **You have strong credit** — Refinancing rates are credit-based. You typically need a credit score above 680 and stable income to qualify for competitive rates.

---

## When refinancing is the wrong move

Do not refinance if:

- You work for a government agency, public school, or 501(c)(3) nonprofit — PSLF could forgive your entire remaining balance after 10 years.
- Your income is low relative to your debt — IDR plans cap payments at a percentage of discretionary income, which could be much lower than a private refinance payment.
- You're enrolled in SAVE or planning to enroll in IBR — even with the current injunction, these plans are likely to resume and could still provide significant payment relief.
- Your employment or income is uncertain — federal loans give you the right to pause payments in hardship; private loans rarely do.

---

## How to compare rates

If you've decided refinancing is right for you, compare offers from multiple lenders before committing. Look at:

- **APR** (not just the headline rate) — includes fees
- **Fixed vs. variable** — variable rates are lower now but can rise
- **Repayment term** — longer terms mean lower payments but more total interest
- **Prepayment penalties** — most reputable lenders have none

**ELFI** (Education Loan Finance) is one lender worth comparing. They specialize in student loan refinancing and offer competitive rates for borrowers with strong credit profiles.

---

## The bottom line

Refinancing makes sense for a narrow group: high-income private sector borrowers with stable careers who will pay off their loans before any forgiveness applies. For everyone else — especially public servants, borrowers with high debt-to-income ratios, or anyone uncertain about their employment — the federal protections you give up are almost certainly worth more than the rate reduction.
